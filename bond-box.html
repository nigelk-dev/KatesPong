Try these

1. Melt the ice cube

Drag the temperature up slowly past 0 °C. Watch the neat pattern break. What happens to the shape of the cube?

What should I see?

The bonds stop holding particles in fixed spots, so the cube slumps into a puddle across the floor. A liquid takes the shape of its container.

2. Make tea on Mount Everest

Tap “Top of Everest”. The air pressure up there is only a third of what it is at the beach. Now find the temperature where the water boils.

What's the answer?

About 71 °C. Less air is pushing down on the water, so particles escape more easily. Climbers' tea is never properly hot.

3. Use a pressure cooker

Tap “Pressure cooker” and heat the water past 100 °C. Is it boiling yet?

Why does this help cooking?

Squeezing the water keeps it liquid until about 120 °C. Hotter water cooks food faster.

4. Strong bonds, weak bonds

Switch between nitrogen and iron. Look at their melting and boiling points. Which one has the stronger bonds?

Check your idea

Iron. Its atoms are held by strong metallic bonds, so it needs 1538 °C just to melt. Nitrogen's molecules pull on each other so weakly that it boils at −196 °C, which is why it's a gas in the air you breathe.

Good to know

Temperature is speed. When something gets hotter, its particles move faster on average. Cold particles are slow, hot ones zoom.

What the lines mean. For water, each dot is a whole H₂O molecule. The lines are the pulls between neighbouring molecules, called hydrogen bonds. When water boils, these break, but the molecules themselves stay whole. Steam is still H₂O.

Melting takes energy. While ice melts, it stays at 0 °C until all of it has turned to water. The heat goes into breaking bonds instead of speeding particles up.

Real numbers are huge. A glass of water holds about 8 million million million million molecules (8 × 10²⁴). This box shows just 100, drawn flat.
